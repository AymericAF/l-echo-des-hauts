::: texte
On the water bill of a Chastel-Ambre household there is a single line giving a price per cubic metre. Behind that line sit five separate authorities, none of which has the power to tell the other four what to do.

This is not a local oddity: it is what the law looks like after thirty years of successive transfers of competence. But on a plateau of 17,400 people and 14 villages, the stack produces one precise effect, visible in each of the four instalments that follow — whenever a decision has to be taken quickly, nobody can take it alone.
:::

::: texte
## The chain, tier by tier

The **intake** belongs to the villages, individually, for the springs they have always owned. **Treatment** — turning raw water into drinking water — has been an inter-municipal responsibility since 2018. **Distribution**, meaning the network and the meters, is too, but on a separate budget that does not merge with the previous one.

**Sewerage** sits with a joint board covering more than the plateau, inherited from a catchment-based boundary. And **water policing** — permitting, restricting, sanctioning — is a state responsibility, exercised by a county-level service.

Five tiers, four budgets, two geographic boundaries that do not line up.
:::

::: encadre titre="Who does what, one sentence each" variante=info
- **Village** : owns the intake and the historic works.
- **Hauts authority** : produces drinking water and runs the network.
- **Catchment board** : handles sewerage, over an area wider than the plateau.
- **Dam operator** : runs the reservoir under a rule written in 1961.
- **State, water policing** : authorises abstraction and issues restrictions.
:::

::: citation auteur="A plateau mayor, clerk of the 2018 session" source="Minutes of the water committee, November 2025"
The day something has to be decided quickly, we will find out that none of us holds the pen. It has been in the statutes since 2018, and nobody read it that way.
:::

::: texte
## What it changes when nothing is wrong

Nothing, and that is the heart of it. While the resource is plentiful the stack is invisible: everyone exercises their competence, the bills go out, the water runs. The chain only becomes visible at the moment it has to contract.

That is what happened in August 2025, when Lake Fauge fell to 41 % of capacity — the lowest reading since 1976. Three villages asked for a release; the dam operator refused. The second instalment of this series tells that refusal, the rule that required it, and why the village learned about it by telephone.
:::
