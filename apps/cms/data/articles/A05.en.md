::: texte
On the water bill of a Chastel-Ambre household there is a single line giving a price per cubic metre. Behind that line sit five separate authorities, none of which has the power to tell the other four what to do.

This is not a local oddity: it is what the law looks like after thirty years of successive transfers of competence. But on a plateau of 17,400 people and 14 villages, the stack produces one precise effect, visible in each of the four instalments that follow — whenever a decision has to be taken quickly, nobody can take it alone.
:::

::: texte
## The chain, tier by tier

The **intake** belongs to the villages, individually, for the springs they have always owned. **Treatment** — turning raw water into drinking water — has been an inter-municipal responsibility since 2018. **Distribution**, meaning the network and the meters, is too, but on a separate budget that does not merge with the previous one.

**Sewerage** sits with a joint board covering more than the plateau, inherited from a catchment-based boundary. And **water policing** — permitting, restricting, sanctioning — is a state responsibility, exercised by a county-level service.

Five tiers, four budgets, two geographic boundaries that do not line up.

A sixth actor fits none of these boxes and yet decides how much water there is: the operator of the Fauge reservoir, who runs the works under a rule written when it was commissioned, in 1961. He is neither elected nor an administration: he applies a document, and that document is not debated in session.
:::

::: encadre titre="Who does what, one sentence each" variante=info
- **Village** : owns the intake and the historic works.
- **Hauts authority** : produces drinking water and runs the network.
- **Catchment board** : handles sewerage, over an area wider than the plateau.
- **Dam operator** : runs the reservoir under a rule written in 1961.
- **State, water policing** : authorises abstraction and issues restrictions.
:::

::: texte
## What each tier takes from the same bill

The price paid per cubic metre breaks down, and the breakdown is published every year in the report on the price and quality of the service. Nobody reads it, because it sits in a table at the back of the document.

It says this. On a plateau household's bill, a little over a third pays for treatment and distribution — the Hauts authority's share, the only one an annual vote actually decides. About a third goes to sewerage, that is to say to a board whose delegates are not elected by plateau residents but appointed by the member authorities. The rest is split between the water agency levies, passed on to the state, and the residual village share attached to the historic intakes.

Put another way, a village voting its water budget votes on neither the price of sewerage, nor the levies, nor the dam rule. It votes on a little over a third of what its residents pay. And the water budget is a **separate** budget: it has to balance on its own revenue, with no transfer from the general budget — which rules out absorbing a bad year elsewhere and forces it onto the price or onto the works programme.

Two consequences, both of which return in the four instalments that follow. Network investment can only be financed by the price, so by an unpopular vote or by a postponement. And a cost decided in a hurry — valves, a standby rota, a flushing operation — lands in that budget, not in the authority's general one.
:::

The day something has to be decided quickly, we will find out that none of us holds the pen. It has been in the statutes since 2018, and nobody read it that way.
:::

::: texte
## What it changes when nothing is wrong

Nothing, and that is the heart of it. While the resource is plentiful the stack is invisible: everyone exercises their competence, the bills go out, the water runs. The chain only becomes visible at the moment it has to contract.

It also has two permanent blind spots, which no period of stress reveals because they sit outside the network. The seven market gardens on the lower Ambre are connected to nothing: they abstract from the river and from three hillside reservoirs, so no decision of the authority reaches them — neither to constrain them nor to protect them. And a family at Pierrefeuille has been drinking its own spring for four generations, with no meter and no account. On paper these are marginal exceptions. What they really show is that the chain of competence does not cover every use of the plateau's water, only those that travel down a public pipe.

That is what happened in August 2025, when Lake Fauge fell to 41 % of capacity — the lowest reading since 1976. Three villages asked for a release; the dam operator refused. The second instalment of this series tells that refusal, the rule that required it, and why the village learned about it by telephone.
:::
