::: texte
The workshop register of the La Rive-Haute mill covers 1971 to 1983. The last page carries the date of 14 June, a column of hourly readings, and one line of handwriting that stops halfway.

This piece does not say what the closure means. It tells the day, in order, from three sources that corroborate one another: the register, the production book, and six people who were there. The political reading belongs to the four instalments that follow.
:::

::: texte
## 5.50 a.m. — the morning shift

The morning shift clocks in at 5.50, as on any other day. For that date the production book carries an unchanged target. Nothing in the documents distinguishes the start of this day from an ordinary Monday.

The closing date had been known for six weeks. What all six accounts agree on is that output was held to the end, and that nobody was told to slow down.
:::

::: citation auteur="A winder, started in 1974" source="Interview, March 2026"
We knew. We did the movements anyway. It is what we knew how to do, and there was no other way to finish.
:::

## 12.10 p.m. — the bailiff comes through

At 12.10 the register carries a handwritten note in a hand different from the rest of the page. It is the only outside intervention recorded that day.

Two of the six accounts place it in the canteen, two in the foreman's office, and the last two do not remember. That disagreement is kept as it stands: forty-three years later, there is no reason to settle it.
:::

## 5.40 p.m. — the last machine

The production book stops at 5.40. That is the time on the final line, followed by a daily total that was never carried over.

Four of the six accounts give the same time without having seen the document. The other two say "around six". It is the only piece of that day on which written and spoken sources agree so precisely.
:::

::: citation auteur="A foreman, started in 1969" source="Interview, March 2026"
There was no speech. Someone cut the power, and the noise stopped. It is the silence we all kept, not the time.
:::
